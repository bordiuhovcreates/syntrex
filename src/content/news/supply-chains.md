A reliable supply chain is not built on a single perfect route. It is built on clear processes, good information and the ability to adapt when something goes wrong. Here are the strategies we see making the biggest difference for companies that move goods internationally.

## Start with a clear picture of your flows

Many efficiency problems begin with incomplete information. Before changing carriers or negotiating new rates, map where your goods actually come from, where they go and how long each stage takes. Even a simple overview of volumes, lanes and average transit times often reveals quick wins: shipments that could be consolidated, routes with unnecessary handovers or suppliers that regularly ship late.

## Consolidate where it makes sense

Shipping many small consignments is convenient, but rarely efficient. Combining orders into fuller containers or pallets lowers the cost per unit, reduces handling and cuts emissions. The key is to agree on fixed shipping days with suppliers, so that consolidation becomes a routine rather than an exception.

## Build in flexibility, not just buffers

Extra stock can protect against delays, but it also ties up cash and warehouse space. A more efficient approach is to keep alternatives ready:

- a second carrier or service on your most important lanes;
- an agreed back-up port or border crossing;
- clear rules for when to switch to a faster mode of transport.

> Resilience is not about predicting every disruption. It is about knowing in advance what you will do when one happens.

## Share information early

Most delays become expensive because they are discovered too late. Sharing forecasts, production schedules and order changes with your logistics partner gives everyone time to react. In return, real-time tracking and early alerts from the transport side let your team adjust plans before customers are affected.

## Measure what matters

Track a small set of indicators consistently: on-time delivery, transit time, cost per unit and the number of exceptions. Reviewing them every month turns gut feeling into facts and shows where improvements actually pay off.

Efficient supply chains are rarely the result of one big change. They come from steady, practical improvements — and from working with partners who treat your cargo as their own.
